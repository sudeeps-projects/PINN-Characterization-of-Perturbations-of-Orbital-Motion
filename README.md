Characterizing Orbital Perturbations via Physics-Informed Neural Networks
An Inverse-Problem Approach to Trajectory Reconstruction and Physical Parameter Discovery from Sparse Observation Data

Abstract
Satellites in low Earth orbit (LEO) depart from ideal Keplerian motion because of Earth’s oblateness (J₂) and atmospheric drag, and drag is the dominant source of LEO orbit-prediction uncertainty because it depends on poorly known upper-atmospheric density and each satellite’s ballistic coefficient. We study the resulting inverse problem: from sparse, noisy observations of a satellite’s state, simultaneously reconstruct the continuous trajectory and recover the hidden drag parameter that produced it. We build a synthetic LEO orbit with a known drag coefficient — integrated under two-body gravity, J₂, and a drag-like term — and sample it into 72 sparse noisy observations (≈3% of the trajectory); we ingest real ISS data (CelesTrak two-line elements propagated with SGP4) and a NASA JPL Horizons benchmark into one schema. A physics-informed neural network (PINN) maps time to the six-dimensional state, combining a sparse-data loss with the residual of the equations of motion evaluated by automatic differentiation, while the drag coefficient is a trainable parameter. A plain network at matched width and depth fails to reconstruct multi-orbit trajectories (RMSE ≈ 8,500 km); tuning it across eleven configurations reaches 131 km at best, so limited capacity contributes alongside spectral bias. adding Fourier-feature time embeddings cuts synthetic error to 0.78 km (≈10,000×) and reconstructs the real ISS orbit to 5.68 km, beating a classical baseline (16.57 km). Parameter recovery is harder: because the instantaneous drag acceleration is only ≈5×10⁻⁵ of gravity, the jointly-learned coefficient errs ≈72%, a frozen-trajectory least-squares estimate ≈29%, and an integrating grid search 0.004%. The PINN excels at sparse-data reconstruction, while weak perturbation parameters remain identifiable only through their accumulated, secular effect.

1. Introduction
Satellites in low Earth orbit do not follow ideal two-body Keplerian paths. Their trajectories are continuously deflected by perturbations, the most important of which are Earth’s oblateness (the J₂ zonal-harmonic term) and atmospheric drag. Drag is the largest source of uncertainty in LEO orbit prediction because it depends on quantities that are poorly measured and highly variable: the density of the upper atmosphere, which responds strongly to solar and geomagnetic activity, and the satellite’s ballistic coefficient B = C_D A / m, which couples the drag coefficient, cross-sectional area, and mass. Real satellites are observed only sparsely and with noise, so recovering physical information from limited tracking is a genuinely hard and operationally relevant problem.
This project addresses the resulting inverse problem: given sparse, noisy observations of a satellite’s state, (a) reconstruct the continuous trajectory and (b) recover the hidden perturbation strength that produced it. With tens of thousands of objects now crowding LEO, conjunction assessment and reentry prediction are limited by drag uncertainty, so a method that extracts physical parameters from few observations has real value for space situational awareness.
In machine-learning terms, this is a supervised, physics-constrained regression cast as an inverse problem. The data are numerical time series — position and velocity state vectors sampled at irregular, sparse times — with no categorical labelling. The model output is twofold: a continuous-valued trajectory (a function from time to a six-dimensional state) and a single scalar physical parameter (the drag or ballistic coefficient). Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [1] are a natural fit: embedding the equations of motion directly in the loss makes them data-efficient, keeps reconstructions physically consistent between observations, and lets unknown coefficients be learned as trainable parameters. PINNs and related neural methods have recently been applied to orbit determination and prediction across LEO, GEO, and cislunar regimes [4]–[8], [13]. What makes the problem scientifically interesting is that the network is asked to discover the physics, returning an interpretable quantity rather than a black-box prediction.

2. Background and Related Work
Foundations: PINNs and spectral bias. Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis [1] introduced PINNs, which add the residual of a governing differential equation to the loss so that a network simultaneously fits data and obeys physics; their strength is data efficiency and the natural handling of inverse problems by treating unknown coefficients as trainable parameters. A known weakness is that standard fully-connected networks exhibit a spectral bias — they learn low-frequency functions far more readily than high-frequency ones (Rahaman et al. [3]) — and Tancik et al. [2] showed that mapping a low-dimensional input through a bank of sinusoidal Fourier features restores the missing high-frequency capacity. Because an eight-orbit LEO trajectory is a high-frequency target in time, these two results turn out to be decisive in this work: a plain network fails, and Fourier-feature embeddings fix it (Section 5).
PINNs for orbit determination and prediction. Varey et al. [4] applied PINNs to satellite state estimation from angles-only observations of a near-geosynchronous (GEO) satellite carrying an unmodeled low-amplitude anomalous acceleration; the network fits the observations and recovers the acceleration profile, and when propagated forward it outperforms the physics-only model by as much as two orders of magnitude over a 20-day horizon (trained on a 48-hour arc) — evidence that the physics term aids extrapolation, which our reconstruction results echo. Loshelder, Sood, and Su [5] and Scorsoglio et al. [6] build PINN orbit-determination pipelines (cislunar and geostationary, respectively) in which unmodeled dynamics such as maneuvers and third-body or solar-radiation-pressure effects are absorbed as learnable terms, directly analogous to our learnable drag coefficient; the GEO pipeline [6] also reprocesses failed cases by retuning hyperparameters, underscoring how delicate PINN training can be. Closest to our LEO setting, Zhao et al. [8] train a physics-informed ResNet for orbit prediction under J₂ and report both lower prediction loss than a non-physics-informed network and large speedups over numerical integration, while Alesiani et al. [7] model a LEO trajectory as a PDE solved with a deep neural operator and benchmark this physics-informed predictor against a pure data-driven model and a numerical simulator — the same baseline-comparison philosophy adopted here. A broader body of work applies neural networks to orbit determination without an explicit physics residual, from GEO and angles-only initial orbit determination [24], [25] to neural replacements for the in-orbit extended Kalman filter [26], [27]; these achieve filter-level accuracy at reduced cost but do not return interpretable physical parameters. PINNs have likewise been used for optimal orbit transfers and low-thrust rendezvous guidance [14], [15], though those target control rather than estimation.
Parameter recovery and inverse problems. The capability most relevant to our central claim is recovering hidden physical parameters from a trajectory. H. M. et al. [9] use a PINN to estimate the trajectory, mass, and coefficient of restitution of untracked debris after an inelastic collision, outperforming classical optimization and standard deep networks — strong evidence that PINNs can recover physical parameters when those parameters leave a strong signature. This is an instructive contrast to our case: a collision imparts a large, abrupt velocity change, whereas atmospheric drag is a weak, slowly-accumulating perturbation, so (as we show) the drag coefficient is only weakly identifiable from an instantaneous physics residual. Besnard et al. [10] use simulation-driven PINNs for nonlinear inverse problems including orbit restitution and report gains over standard PINNs, supporting our synthetic-first methodology, and Heidrich and Holzinger [11] show that a sparse-grid-collocation formulation of orbit determination has a much larger region of convergence and remains stable across measurement gaps of hours to days, where numerical-shooting methods break down — motivating our collocation-based physics loss for sparse observations. Finally, Badura et al. [12] study convergence and uncertainty for physics-informed orbit determination and find accuracy sensitive to the initial guess, consistent with the training delicacy we observe.
Classical baseline and our positioning. Traditional astrodynamics (Vallado [16]; King-Hele [17]) estimates drag by differential correction or batch least squares — integrating an orbit from an assumed state and adjusting the ballistic coefficient until the propagated trajectory matches observations — and the operational SGP4 model and two-line element sets [18], [19] encode an effective drag term (B*) for every catalogued object. We implement this classical idea directly as a grid-search baseline, which recovers the true synthetic coefficient to 0.004%. Relative to the literature above, our contribution is twofold: we target multi-orbit LEO reconstruction from sparse, noisy data and identify spectral bias as the dominant failure mode, fixing it with Fourier features; and we provide an honest analysis of when a weak perturbation parameter is and is not identifiable from an instantaneous physics residual, comparing three estimators on a problem with known ground truth.

3. Dataset
All data are numerical state-vector time series (position in km, velocity in km/s, with an associated timestamp). The project uses three complementary sources, deliberately ordered from a fully controlled simulation to authentic tracking data.
3.1 Synthetic dataset (primary, ground-truth known)
Ground-truth orbits are generated by numerically integrating the equations of motion with an adaptive Runge–Kutta solver (SciPy solve_ivp [23], RK45, relative and absolute tolerances 10⁻⁹) under two-body gravity, the J₂ oblateness term, and an exponential-atmosphere drag term whose hidden coefficient is fixed at 3.245×10⁻⁵. The reference orbit is a 500 km-altitude, 35°-inclination circular LEO with an orbital period of 94.6 min, propagated for eight orbital periods (12.6 h) and sampled at 2,500 dense states. Because the data are self-generated, the true parameter is known exactly, which makes honest evaluation of the inverse problem possible.
Pre-processing. The dense truth is down-sampled to every 35th state (72 sparse observations, ≈2.9% of the trajectory) and corrupted with Gaussian noise (0.5 km on each position component, 2 m/s on each velocity component) to mimic realistic tracking error. All products are exported as reproducible CSV files with a metadata table. The physical significance of the perturbations is shown in Figure 1: over 12.6 h, J₂ alone drives the orbit 762 km away from a pure two-body path, while J₂-plus-drag drives it 2,944 km away — so the drag term contributes roughly 2,180 km of accumulated, secular drift. That accumulated drift is precisely the signal the inverse problem must exploit, and Figure 2 shows the sparse noisy observations against the dense truth.

Figure 1. Growth of the position residual relative to pure two-body motion over eight orbits. J₂ contributes ≈762 km of drift; adding the drag-like perturbation raises this to ≈2,944 km. The ≈2,180 km drag-induced difference is the secular signal that makes the hidden coefficient identifiable.


   
3.2 Real-data branch (authenticity)
General-perturbations (GP) records for the International Space Station (NORAD catalog ID 25544) are downloaded live from CelesTrak [28]; for the runs reported here the TLE epoch is 18 June 2026, 19:14 UTC, with inclination 51.63°, eccentricity 4.67×10⁻⁴, mean motion 15.493 rev/day, and drag term B* = 1.54×10⁻⁴. The TLE is propagated with SGP4 [18] into Cartesian states (5-minute steps over 12 h, 145 dense states spanning altitudes of 412–424 km), then down-sampled to 25 sparse observations at 30-minute spacing with 200 m position and 0.2 m/s velocity noise. A caveat is stated throughout the project: TLE/SGP4 output is a real-data-derived benchmark, not raw measurement data, which is appropriate for a first study but should be replaced by raw tracking in future work. Figure 3 shows the propagated ISS altitude profile.

Figure 3. Altitude over time for the real ISS orbit, obtained by propagating a live CelesTrak TLE (epoch 18 June 2026) with SGP4. The orbit oscillates between ≈412 and ≈424 km.

3.3 Benchmark branch and unified schema
High-precision geocentric state vectors for the Moon are retrieved from NASA JPL Horizons [29] (73 hourly samples, radius 360,348–364,083 km) to validate the ingestion pipeline against an authoritative source. All three sources — 2,500 synthetic states, 145 ISS states, and 73 Horizons states — are converted into one unified schema (UTC timestamp, elapsed seconds, position in km, velocity in km/s, and derived radius, speed, and altitude), so that every later experiment runs on a common format.

3.4 Splits
Training uses only the sparse noisy observations (72 synthetic / 25 real). The label-free physics loss is evaluated at collocation times (400 synthetic / 200 real) spanning the window. The full dense trajectory (2,500 / 145 states) is held out purely as the test set for reconstruction error, and validation monitors the loss components and the convergence of the learned parameter. Because the synthetic ground truth and its hidden coefficient are known exactly, test-set reconstruction RMSE and parameter error are both true, unbiased measures of performance.

4. Methodology and Models
4.1 Forward physical model
The state is x(t) = [r, v] with position r and velocity v. The dynamics are first-order:
dr/dt = v,  dv/dt = a₂₋ᵇᵒᵈʸ(r) + a_J₂(r) + a_drag(r, v; θ).
The two-body term is a₂₋ᵇᵒᵈʸ = −μ r / |r|³ with μ = 398,600.44 km³/s². The oblateness term a_J₂ uses the standard zonal-harmonic expression with J₂ = 1.08263×10⁻³ and Earth radius 6,378.14 km. (An incorrect J₂ value of 1.31×10⁻³ present in an early version of the data-generation code was corrected to the standard value so that the data-generating physics and the PINN residual use identical constants.) For the synthetic case the drag-like acceleration is a_drag = −θ·exp(−(|r|−Rₑ)/H)·|v|·v with scale height H = 60 km and hidden coefficient θ. For the real-data case a more physical model is used: a_drag = −½·ρ(h)·B·|v_rel|·v_rel, where ρ(h) is an exponential atmosphere, v_rel is the velocity relative to the co-rotating atmosphere (Ω_⊕ = 7.292×10⁻⁵ rad/s), and B = C_D A / m is the ballistic coefficient (prior assumptions C_D = 2.2, A = 1000 m², m = 420,000 kg, giving B ≈ 5.24×10⁻³ m²/kg).

4.2 Classical inverse baseline
Assuming the initial state is known, the baseline scans 40 candidate drag coefficients on [0.2, 4.0]×10⁻⁵, numerically integrates the orbit for each candidate, and selects the value minimizing position mean-squared error at the sparse observation times. This is the classical differential-correction idea reduced to a one-parameter grid search, and it provides the accuracy target for the learned model.

4.3 PINN architecture
The model is a multilayer perceptron that maps normalized time t ∈ [0,1] to the normalized six-dimensional state. Two design choices proved essential. First, Fourier-feature embedding [2]: instead of feeding the scalar time directly, time is embedded as γ(t) = [t, sin(2πkt), cos(2πkt)] for k = 1…22, giving a 49-dimensional input. Because an eight-orbit trajectory completes eight high-frequency cycles over the unit interval, this embedding lets a small tanh network (width 128, depth 3) represent motion that a plain network cannot owing to spectral bias [3]. Second, the time and state are normalized for stable optimization, but the physics residual is always computed in physical units after de-normalization.
Loss function. Training minimizes a weighted sum of four terms: (1) a data loss, the mean-squared error between the network and the sparse observations; (2) an initial-condition loss anchoring the trajectory at the known starting state; (3) a physics loss, the mean-squared residual of dr/dt − v and dv/dt − a(r, v; θ) evaluated by automatic differentiation at collocation points (400 synthetic / 200 real), with the kinematic and dynamic residuals divided by characteristic speed and acceleration scales so they contribute comparably; and, for the real-data model, (4) a weak parameter-prior loss keeping log B near its physical prior. Evaluating physics at collocation points, rather than shooting from the initial state, is the same strategy shown to be robust across observation gaps in sparse-grid-collocation orbit determination [11]. The unknown coefficient is learned in log-space (log θ or log B) so it remains strictly positive, mirroring PINN pipelines that learn unmodeled dynamical terms [5], [6].
Optimization and parameter estimators. Training uses Adam [20] (initial learning rate 2×10⁻³) with step-decay scheduling, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, fixed random seeds, and 5,000 epochs, implemented in PyTorch [21] with NumPy [22]. Three drag estimators are compared: the jointly-learned coefficient (optimized together with the network weights); a frozen-trajectory least-squares estimate (after training, the reconstruction is held fixed and the drag coefficient — which enters the dynamics linearly — is solved in closed form, so the network can no longer absorb the drag signature into trajectory adjustments); and the classical integrating grid-search baseline.

5. Results and Discussion
5.1 Perturbation characterization and classical baseline
The forward model confirms that the perturbations are individually subtle but accumulate strongly: over eight orbits the J₂ term produces 762 km of drift relative to two-body motion and the drag term adds a further ≈2,180 km (Figure 1). Against this signal, the classical grid-search baseline recovers a drag coefficient of 3.245×10⁻⁵ versus the true 3.245×10⁻⁵ — a 0.004% relative error — from only 72 noisy observations (Figure 4). This establishes both that the hidden parameter is identifiable in principle and the accuracy bar for the learned model.


Figure 4. Classical inverse baseline: position MSE at the sparse observations versus candidate drag coefficient. The minimum (3.245×10⁻⁵) falls within 0.004% of the true value (3.245×10⁻⁵).


5.2 Trajectory reconstruction: spectral bias and the Fourier fix
The central modeling result is that a plain tanh-MLP PINN cannot reconstruct a multi-orbit trajectory. Its prediction collapses toward the orbit mean (Figure 5, left), giving a position RMSE of 8,515 km on the synthetic test set and 7,944 km on the real ISS orbit — larger than the orbital radius itself. This is the spectral bias of Section 2 [3]: eight orbital cycles are too high-frequency for the network to represent. Adding Fourier-feature time embeddings [2] resolves the failure completely (Figure 5, right): the synthetic reconstruction RMSE drops to 0.78 km (velocity RMSE 1.22 m/s), an improvement of roughly four orders of magnitude, and the reconstruction is visually indistinguishable from the ground truth even between the sparse observations. Table 1 summarizes reconstruction performance.

Table 1. Trajectory reconstruction performance (position RMSE on the held-out dense test set).


On the real ISS orbit the Fourier PINN reconstructs the full state to 5.68 km position RMSE (velocity RMSE 8.0 m/s; final-state error 1.52 km) from just 25 sparse observations (Figure 6). Notably, this beats the classical numerical-integration baseline on the same data (16.57 km RMSE): the PINN denoises and interpolates the sparse, noisy observations more robustly than forward-integrating a single noisy initial state, because the data term constrains the whole window rather than propagating one starting error. This agrees with reports that PINNs fit observations better than physics-only models and extrapolate well [4], and that physics-informed networks outperform their non-physics counterparts under J₂ [8].


Figure 6. Real ISS orbit reconstruction. The Fourier-feature PINN (orange dashed) matches the SGP4 benchmark (blue) through the 25 sparse observations, achieving 5.68 km position RMSE over 12 h.

5.3 Parameter recovery and an identifiability limit
Recovering the hidden drag coefficient is fundamentally harder than reconstructing the trajectory, and the results expose why. At 500 km the instantaneous drag acceleration is only about 5×10⁻⁵ of gravity, so a 0.7 km reconstruction error perturbs the dominant two-body term by more than the entire drag signal. The jointly-learned coefficient therefore settles at 8.81×10⁻⁶ — a 72.9% error — because the flexible network prefers to absorb the small drag mismatch into tiny trajectory adjustments rather than into the coefficient (the parameter barely moves from its initial guess; Figure 7). Heavier physics weighting does not rescue it: in additional experiments it either left the coefficient stuck or destroyed the reconstruction, an instance of the initialization- and weighting-sensitivity reported for physics-informed orbit determination [12].
The frozen-trajectory least-squares estimator improves this substantially. Once the reconstruction is fixed, the drag signal can no longer be hidden in trajectory wiggles, and the closed-form fit recovers 2.297×10⁻⁵ — a 29.2% error, correct in both order of magnitude and sign. The classical trajectory-integrating baseline remains far more accurate (0.004%) because it uses the full ≈2,000 km of accumulated, secular drift from a known initial state rather than the instantaneous, pointwise residual. Table 2 collects the three estimates. The contrast with H. M. et al. [9], who recover a debris object’s mass and coefficient of restitution with a PINN, is instructive: a collision imparts a large, abrupt signature that is strongly identifiable, whereas a weak drag perturbation over a short arc is not — identifiability, not the PINN machinery, is the deciding factor.

Real-data parameter recovery. For the real ISS orbit, the learned ballistic coefficient stays essentially at its prior (ratio 1.0000002). This is the physically correct outcome, not a failure: over a 12-hour window the ISS loses only a negligible amount of altitude, so its decay carries almost no information about B, and the prior dominates. Identifying a real ballistic coefficient would require either a much longer observation arc (days to weeks of TLE history) or an object with a larger area-to-mass ratio.
5.4 Error analysis and limitations
Weak-parameter identifiability. The instantaneous physics residual cannot constrain a perturbation whose pointwise acceleration is below the reconstruction error. Parameter recovery should use the accumulated/secular effect (as the integrating baseline and the frozen-trajectory estimator partially do).
Real-data fidelity. TLE/SGP4 states are an estimated, model-smoothed product, not raw measurements; the reported real-data results characterize how well the method reconstructs that benchmark, not raw tracking error.
Density model. The exponential atmosphere is an educational approximation; real density varies by large factors with solar and geomagnetic activity, and density and B are partially degenerate, so only their product is identifiable.
Single configuration. Results are reported for one orbit geometry and one noise level per branch; broader sweeps over altitude, area-to-mass ratio, sparsity, and noise would strengthen the conclusions.

6. Conclusion
This project framed LEO orbit characterization as an inverse problem — reconstruct the trajectory and recover the hidden drag parameter from sparse, noisy observations — and tested it first on a fully controlled synthetic orbit with known ground truth, then on real ISS data, all expressed in a single unified schema. The headline result is that physics-informed neural networks are excellent sparse-data reconstructors once spectral bias is addressed: a plain network fails badly (RMSE ≈ 8,500 km), but Fourier-feature time embeddings reduce synthetic reconstruction error to 0.78 km and real-ISS error to 5.68 km, the latter beating a classical numerical-integration baseline. The model performs well here because the data loss anchors the trajectory at 72 (or 25) points while the physics loss regularizes the gaps, and because Fourier features give the small network the capacity to represent many orbital cycles.
The more nuanced finding concerns parameter discovery. The hidden drag coefficient is only weakly identifiable from an instantaneous residual — its acceleration is ≈10⁻⁵ of gravity — so the jointly-learned value is biased (≈72% error), while a frozen-trajectory least-squares estimate (≈29%) and especially a trajectory-integrating grid search (0.004%) do far better by exploiting the accumulated drift. In other words, the project’s central claim — that sparse observations plus physics constraints can recover hidden physical information — holds, but with the important qualification that weak perturbation parameters are identifiable through their secular, accumulated effect rather than their pointwise signature. This complements prior PINN parameter-recovery successes on strongly-identifiable quantities [9] by mapping the regime where the approach reaches its limit.
Next steps. The most valuable extensions are: (1) reformulate parameter recovery around the accumulated trajectory — for example a neural-ODE or trajectory-matching loss, or a residual-physics design in which the network learns only the unmodeled drag acceleration on top of an exact gravity-plus-J₂ propagation, as Varey et al. [4] do for an anomalous thrust — so the PINN itself recovers drag accurately; (2) replace the exponential atmosphere with an operational density model (NRLMSISE-00 or JB2008) including solar and geomagnetic drivers; (3) use multi-day Space-Track TLE histories so a real ballistic coefficient becomes identifiable; (4) compare objects across altitude and area-to-mass regimes (e.g., ISS, Starlink, Iridium); and (5) run systematic ablations over sparsity, noise, and physics weighting.
